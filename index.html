02 Projects

Palomares Lab · with Dr. Emily Schwitzgebel

First-author manuscript under review at PLOS ONE

Expectations, context, and human judgment

A study of how source labels influence responses to AI-generated music.

The question

Plain language

We investigated whether listeners could tell human songs apart from matched AI-generated versions of them, and how knowing a song’s source affected the way they evaluated it.

Scientific detail

For each human song, my Python pipeline extracted acoustic and lyric features and turned them into prompts, and a matched AI version was generated from those prompts in Suno. We compared listeners’ judgments when the source was hidden and when it was labeled.

A follow-up study, where I lead stimulus preparation and the Qualtrics build, includes comparisons involving Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini. It tests whether genre prototypicality helps explain AI music’s performance in blind comparisons. That is a hypothesis under investigation.

What we found

Listeners couldn’t distinguish the human songs from their AI-generated counterparts. They preferred the AI songs when the source was hidden, and the human songs when the source was labeled.

These results describe this study, not listeners or music in general.

Why it interests me

This work made me more interested in the expectations and experiences people bring to a judgment. That interest is also part of why I’m drawn to medicine: I want to understand both the biology of illness and the person experiencing it—their concerns, beliefs, and what matters to them.

Palomares Lab · my software contribution

A Python pipeline for matched AI music

A tool that analyzes human songs and turns their features into prompts for matched AI versions.

  1. Extract acoustic and lyric features (tempo, rhythm, key, timbre, dynamics, vocal range).
  2. Turn those features into prompts.
  3. Generate matched AI versions in Suno.
  4. Prepare the stimuli for listener studies.

This is the tool behind the stimuli for “Expectations, context, and human judgment.” The study’s conclusions come from listener data, not the software.
