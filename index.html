Featured study · Palomares Lab

How source labels shape music judgments

A study of how identifying music as human-created or AI-generated affects listeners’ evaluations.

First-author manuscript · under review at PLOS ONE

With Dr. Emily Schwitzgebel (School of Music) · Supervisor Dr. Melanie Palomares

Research question

Can listeners distinguish human-created songs from AI-generated versions designed to match them? How does revealing the source affect their evaluations?

Key finding

  • Source hidden

    AI-generated songs preferred

  • Source disclosed

    Human-created songs preferred

Within this study’s sample and song set, listeners also did not reliably distinguish the human-created songs from their AI-generated counterparts.

My role

  • Co-led the listener study and am first author on the manuscript.
  • Developed the Python pipeline that generates the prompts for the AI-generated versions.
  • Lead stimulus preparation and develop the Qualtrics experiment for a follow-up study.

Method

  1. Human-created songs
  2. AI-generated versionsdesigned to match; generated in Suno from pipeline prompts
  3. Listener studysource hidden or disclosed
  4. Compare evaluationssource-identification accuracy; listener preferences

Plain language

Listeners evaluated human-created songs and AI-generated versions designed to share selected musical features. The study compared their evaluations with the source hidden and with the source disclosed.

Scientific detail

For each human-created song, my pipeline extracted acoustic and lyric features and converted them into text prompts; Suno, an AI music generator, produced a version from each prompt.

The follow-up study tests a hypothesis: that genre prototypicality (how closely a song resembles a typical example of its genre) helps explain how AI-generated songs perform when listeners don’t know the source.

Why it interests me

This research taught me that a judgment is never just about the stimulus. It’s also about the person making it: their history, expectations, and values. Illness works the same way. I’m drawn to medicine because I want to understand both the biology of disease and the human experience of it, and to care for patients in a way that respects both.

Wiskur Lab · Organic Chemistry

Silane polymers and their microenvironments

Enantioselectivity, with applications in pharmaceutical chromatography and contaminated-water cleanup.

Ongoing since October 2025

Focus
I study polymer microenvironments. Our experiments emphasize enantioselectivity (a preference for producing or separating one of a molecule’s two mirror-image forms) and applications of silane polymers in pharmaceutical column chromatography and contaminated-water cleanup.
My contribution
  • Set up and troubleshoot reactions, using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) to monitor progress.
  • Purify products using column chromatography, extraction, recrystallization, and distillation.
  • Characterize products using ¹H and ¹³C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.
  • Plan experiments with appropriate controls.
